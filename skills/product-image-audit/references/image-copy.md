# Grading the copy on the images

The words on the images matter as much as the pictures. Read every word on every slide (open the image, don't trust alt text) and grade it.

## The ladder (grade every line of on-image copy)

1. **Feature (weakest).** Says what the product has. "Soft & chewy." "60/40 cotton blend." "Under 2 kcal."
2. **Benefit (better).** Says what that does for the buyer. "Won't crumble in your pocket." "Doesn't shrink in the wash."
3. **End result (best).** Ties the benefit to the buyer's real goal, the thing they bought the product to get. "Irresistible scent and taste helps motivate your dog to learn new tricks." The dog owner's goal isn't a treat. It's a dog that listens.

For each slide, name its rung and, if it's not on rung 3, write a better line that climbs one or two rungs. Keep a feature only when the next words say why it matters ("Under 2 kcal, so you can reward 50 times without overfeeding").

Work out the buyer's end goal first, in one line, before grading anything ("a dog that behaves on walks", "lips that look done without a mirror", "a tee that looks sharp untucked"). Every rewrite should point there.

## Psychology checks (top-tier copy)

Check each slide's copy for these. Name the technique when a slide uses one well, and name the missing one when you rewrite.

- **One message per slide.** One clear claim a shopper gets in a one-second look on a phone. Slides that each carry "one little piece of proof" beat one slide carrying six.
- **Specific beats vague.** Numbers, names, concrete pictures ("smaller than a penny", "used by 1,200 trainers") beat adjectives ("high quality", "premium").
- **Claims need proof.** Every claim on slide 1 or 2 ("all natural", "low calorie", "clinically shown") should be backed somewhere in the gallery: a facts panel, a test result, a review. An unproved claim reads as marketing.
- **The buyer, not the brand.** "You/your dog" lines beat "we/our" lines. Copy written from the buyer's side of the table.
- **Aspiration and identity.** Lines that let the buyer see the person (or pet) they want to be ("the dog everyone at the park asks about").
- **Objection handling and FAQs.** The buyer's worries and practical questions (see category-needs.md) answered in words, not just shown. A plain answer to a real question ("Induction-safe", "Made without PTFE, PFOA, PFAS") is top-tier copy even though it reads like a feature, because the buyer asked that question. Credit it and leave it alone. Rewrite only if the answer is vague ("Consciously crafted coating" doesn't answer "what is it made of?").
- **Social proof in words.** Real quotes, real counts, real names, not slogans.
- **Contrast.** "Not X, Y" or vs-the-alternative lines that make the difference obvious in a crowded category.
- **Plain names.** Branded names (a shade called "First Move", a collection name) must say what they are ("Shade: First Move, nude pink"). Big text that means nothing to a new visitor is wasted.
- **Throwaway slogans.** Rhymes and taglines that say nothing ("Training made easy, treats made right") are the weakest slides. Flag them and write a replacement.
- **Wordiness.** More than ~8 words of claim text per block, or more than 4 bullets, won't be read on a phone. Suggest the cut.

## Slide 1 copy

Slide 1 should make the product obvious AND give one reason to buy: the unique value proposition, or the live promotion. Cold traffic needs both. A clean studio shot with 2-3 short benefit words on it (Pupford: "All natural / High value / Low calorie") is the model. A running promo on slide 1 is fine, just note it must come off when the promo ends.

## Don't invent proof

Rewrites may only use numbers, quotes and claims that are on the page or given by the user. Otherwise use a placeholder like `[your trainer count]`.
