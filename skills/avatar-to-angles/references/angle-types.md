# Angle types

Twelve types of angle. Each one does a different persuasive job. A good set of five uses at least four types, so the user can test different arguments, not five versions of one argument.

For each type: what it does, which avatar fields it needs, and the shape it usually takes. If the research doesn't have the field a type needs, skip that type. Don't make the missing fact up.

---

**1. Not your fault**
Takes the blame off the reader and puts it on something else (the wrong tool, the wrong advice, how the category works).
Needs: what they blame (field 4) + what they tried (field 6).
Shape: "[Problem]? It's not you. It's [the thing they've been using]."
Why it works: people who've tried and failed feel stupid. Taking that away earns their attention.

**2. The real cause**
Names what's actually behind the problem.
Needs: the real cause (field 5), from the brand or well known facts.
Shape: "The real reason [problem] keeps coming back" / "It's not [assumed cause]. It's [real cause]."

**3. Myth breaker**
Takes a belief they hold and shows it's wrong, or only half true.
Needs: a belief that shows up in the quotes, and a fact that corrects it.
Shape: "[Common belief]? Here's what's actually happening." / "Why [popular fix] doesn't do what people think."
Careful: the correction has to be true. Don't swap one myth for another.

**4. Who it's for (call-out)**
Names a specific group so the right reader thinks "that's me".
Needs: a group the quotes clearly show (field 11).
Shape: "For people over 50 who [problem]" / "If you [specific situation], read this."
Note: naming the group is also a clearer signal to ad platforms about who to show it to.

**5. Tried everything**
Speaks to people who've already tried the usual fixes.
Needs: failed solutions with reasons (field 6).
Shape: "If you've tried [A], [B] and [C] and [problem] is still there..."

**6. Fear killer**
Takes the biggest objection or fear and answers it head on.
Needs: a fear or objection (field 8) the product genuinely answers.
Shape: "How to [get the outcome] without [the thing they're scared of]."
Often the strongest angle for solution-aware buyers, because the fear is the only thing stopping them.

**7. The outcome (how-to)**
Promises the result, plainly.
Needs: what they want (field 7), in their words.
Shape: "How to [outcome] when you're [situation]."
This is often the obvious angle. It wins when it's specific enough that it couldn't belong to another product.

**8. What it's costing you**
Names what they're losing now: money on products that don't work, time, a moment they keep avoiding.
Needs: a real cost in the quotes, or real prices.
Shape: "You've spent [real amount] on [things that didn't work]" / "[Problem] is why you [thing they avoid]."

**9. Price math (cost swap)**
Compares the price to something they already pay for.
Needs: a real price and a real alternative price (a salon visit, a monthly habit).
Shape: "Less than one [expensive alternative]."

**10. Discovery / curiosity**
Something unexpected about the problem or the category that makes them want to know more.
Needs: a real, surprising fact from the research or the brand. No invented studies.
Shape: "What happens when you [change one thing]." / "Why [surprising observation]."

**11. Story**
How a real person (the founder, a named customer the brand can point to) found a way out.
Needs: a real story the brand has. Never a made-up customer.
Shape: "How [person] finally [outcome] after [failed attempts]."

**12. Proof first**
Leads with real proof: a real review quote as the headline, a real number of customers, real press.
Needs: real reviews, numbers or press. A review quote used as a headline must be a real quote.
Shape: "[Real review line]" / "[Real number] [people like them] switched to [category]."
Best for product-aware readers.

## Matching angles to awareness

| Awareness | Angle types that usually fit |
|---|---|
| Problem-aware | Not your fault, real cause, myth breaker, outcome, call-out |
| Solution-aware | Fear killer, tried everything, myth breaker, price math, discovery |
| Product-aware | Proof first, fear killer, story |
| Most aware | Price math, offer (only when there's a real offer) |

## Good angle vs weak angle

A good angle:
- Comes straight out of a quote or a pattern of quotes.
- Could not run for a different product without changing it.
- Has a belief or fear to push against AND a payoff.
- Can be said in one plain sentence.

A weak angle:
- Is a category claim anyone could make ("glowing skin", "better sleep").
- Needs a fact the brand doesn't have.
- Is clever but the reader can't tell what they get.
