# Headlines

Three kinds of headline: advertorial, listicle and ad. They work differently. Don't write an advertorial headline for a listicle or the other way round.

## What every headline needs

1. **Flag the reader.** They recognize themselves at first glance: a group ("people over 50"), or a situation only they live ("when your foundation sits on your peach fuzz"). If it could run for any brand, rewrite it.
2. **Push against something (the backward move).** A belief they hold, a fix they tried, a fear they have. This is what makes it worth reading.
3. **Promise something (the forward move).** The reader can say in one phrase what they'll get by reading on. The promise must be visible, not implied. Three ways to add it:
   - Parentheses: "Why your peach fuzz feels rough after you shave it (and how to avoid it)"
   - A second sentence: "It's not your razor. Here's what's really going on."
   - A colon: "Peach fuzz over 50: the removal mistake that makes it feel worse"
4. **Specific beats clever.** A concrete claim beats wordplay.
5. **Plain words.** Every word a 10-year-old knows. "How much your body actually absorbs", not "bioavailability".
6. **10 to 18 words** for page headlines. Up to 22 only if a parenthetical earns it. Ad headlines can be much shorter.
7. **One breath.** Read it out loud. If you run out of air, cut.
8. **Only promise what the page can deliver.** If delivering the promise would need a study, a test or a number you don't have, pick a different promise.
9. **No made-up numbers.** Any number has to come from the research or the brand.
10. **No em dashes.** Use a colon, period or parentheses.

Vary the shape across candidates: question, statement, "you" address, how-to, number. Five candidates that all start with "Why" or "The" is a fail.

## Advertorial headlines

An advertorial reads like an article. The headline should look like it belongs above a magazine feature, not on an ad. The brand and product are NOT named.

Write 5 candidates, each using a different frame:

1. **The obvious one.** The angle every competitor already runs. Write it first and label it, so the other four are different.
2. **Mistake.** The everyday habit that causes the problem without them realizing. Vary the hedge ("without realizing it", "slowly", "might be").
3. **Who it's for.** Why this group suddenly has this problem. Only when the group is in the research.
4. **Myth breaker.** Why a popular belief or fix doesn't do what people expect.
5. **Real cause.** The overlooked reason behind the problem. Rotate the opener so you don't always use the same one:
   - "The real reason [problem]..."
   - "What's actually behind [problem]..."
   - "It's not [assumed cause]. It's [real cause]."
   - "Why [problem] keeps coming back"
6. **Story.** How a real person found a way out. Only with a real story.
7. **Curiosity.** Something unexpected happens when people change one specific thing.
8. **Discovery.** Researchers found a link between A and B. Only if there is a real, citable study.

Pick the candidate with the most specific, concrete promise. If two are tied, pick the one whose idea is least common in the category's ads.

Subheadline: adds one real detail the headline didn't have, in the reader's language. No numbers, no jargon. It can hint at how the fix works.

**Message match:** if the reader came from an ad, the headline must feel like the next line of that ad, not a new topic.

## Listicle headlines

A listicle is a numbered list of reasons. The headline must start with (or contain) the number of items, and the number must match the item count.

Two kinds:
- **Problem-led:** no brand name. Names the problem, group or outcome.
- **Product-led:** names the brand or product in every candidate.

Pick one and keep all five candidates on it.

Five slots, one candidate each:

1. **The obvious one.** "[N] Reasons [Group] Are Switching to [Category]". Write it, label it.
2. **Benefit inside.** The concrete outcome is in the headline. "[N] Reasons [Group] Use [Category] for [Specific Outcome]"
3. **Problem inside.** The pain is in the headline. "[N] Reasons [People With the Problem] Still Haven't Found a Fix" / "[N] Ways [Common Fix] Makes [Problem] Worse"
4. **Narrow group.** The group is narrow enough that the right reader thinks "this is for me". "[N] Things Every [Specific Group] Should Know About [Category]"
5. **Correction (problem-led)** "[N] Things [Group] Get Wrong About [Category]" / "[N] Signs Your [Current Approach] Isn't Working". **Or proof (product-led)** "Why [N] Out of [M] [Group] Are [Action]" (only with a real number).

Check every verb has its object. "7 Reasons People Over 50 Are Making the Switch": switching to what? Rewrite: "7 Reasons People Over 50 Are Switching to Dermaplaning".

Item titles: each one is a reason that completes "[Headline], because...". If an item can't complete that sentence, it doesn't belong or the headline is wrong. Items are statements, not questions.

Subheadline: points at the most surprising item or reframes the list, so they want to see which ones made it.

## Ad headlines and hooks

Ads are allowed to sound like ads. They're shorter, punchier and can use the direct-response shapes the page headlines avoid. Proven shapes, grouped by job:

**Not your fault / real culprit**
- "[Symptom]? It's not you. It's your [thing they use]."
- "Maybe it was never the [assumed culprit]."
- "Stop using [the usual fix]."
- "Ditch the [old solution]."

**Who it's for**
- "PSA for [specific group]"
- "If you've tried [failed fixes]..."
- "If your [person/pet] has [problem], try this"
- "Made for [underserved group], not [default group]"

**Outcome / speed** (only with a timeframe the brand publishes)
- "[Benefit] without the [downside]"
- "[Result] in [real timeframe]"
- "[Enjoy the thing]. Skip the [downside]."

**Loss / cost**
- "Still [old behavior]?"
- "Lose [bad thing], not [good thing]"
- "Cheaper than one [expensive alternative]" (real prices only)

**Proof** (real proof only)
- "[A real review line as the headline]"
- "From '[real insult]' to '[real compliment]'"
- "[Real number] [group] ditched [old thing] for this"

**Curiosity / myth**
- "Myth: [common belief]"
- "[N] signs of [problem they haven't named]"
- "This is why you can't [desired outcome]:"
- "POV: [relatable moment with the product]"

Not allowed for supplements, food and drink, beauty, pet or fitness: anything that implies a hidden medical problem ("Is your X silently harming you?", "Warning: ...") or a permanent fix ("End X forever", "Never again", "The last X you'll ever need"). "#1" or "best" only with a real ranking or award.

## Final checks (run before handing over)

- [ ] Does each headline flag the reader?
- [ ] Is there a visible promise?
- [ ] Every word plain enough for a 10-year-old?
- [ ] Every number and quote real and from the research?
- [ ] Advertorial and problem-led listicle: no brand name?
- [ ] Listicle: same number in all five, matching the item count, every verb has its object?
- [ ] Five candidates use five different frames and at least three different opening shapes?
- [ ] No em dashes, no clock times, no banned words (see SKILL.md rule 6)?
- [ ] "They", not "she" or "he", when talking about the customer?
- [ ] Would this headline make the avatar think "that's me"? If it would work just as well for a different group, rewrite it.
