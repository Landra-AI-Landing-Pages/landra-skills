# Portfolio review

A list of pages tells you what someone built. A portfolio tells you what they are missing. The page list reports what each page targets and not just its title, so you can see the shape of the whole set in one call. Use it.

## Read the set, not the rows

Group the pages before you say anything about them. Four groupings matter:

- **By reader.** Cluster the pages by the audience each one was written for. Five pages chasing the same reader is the single most common pattern, and it means every ad set is fighting for the same person.
- **By format.** All advertorials, or all listicles, means one whole approach to cold traffic is untested.
- **By template.** All standard means the premium templates have never been tried. All one premium template means the same.
- **By product.** If the brand sells four things and three of them have no page, that is the loudest gap on the board. Nothing reports the product per page, so this one you infer from titles and from what the brand sells. Say that you are inferring it rather than stating it as fact.

## The gap taxonomy

Name gaps in this order. The earlier ones are usually worth more.

**1. Unserved product.** A product with no page at all. Nothing else on this list beats shipping the first page for a product that has none.

**2. Unserved reader.** A real buyer segment with no page written for them. This is where saved research pays off: if the dossier holds four avatars and the pages only cover one, the other three are named, specific gaps rather than guesses. Read the saved research before claiming a reader is unserved.

**3. Untested format.** Advertorial and listicle work differently on cold traffic. Story-driven advertorials suit products that need a mechanism explained or a belief changed. Listicles suit products where the case is cumulative and the reader is comparison shopping. A brand that has only ever run one has no evidence about the other, and finding out costs one page.

**4. Untested angle on a proven product.** When a page is already published and working, the highest-value next page is usually the same product to a different reader, or the same reader through a different objection. Duplicating is the cheap way to test a variant of a page that works: the copy starts as an independent draft rather than inheriting the original's live URL, so it can be edited and published on its own without touching the page already running.

**5. Drafts that never shipped.** Pages sitting in draft, especially ones where images completed. These cost a credit already. Publishing one is free value; ask whether it stalled for a reason.

**6. Stale offer.** Published pages carrying a price, discount or guarantee the brand has since changed. Text edits fix these, but the live page does not update until it is republished. Worth flagging explicitly because it is silent.

## What to hand back

Give a short status board first: one line per page, its state, and the link that matters (live URL when published, editor link otherwise). Then two or three named gaps, ranked, each with the concrete next page it implies. Not a list of everything that could be built.

Anchor recommendations in what the portfolio actually shows. "Four of your five pages are written for the same reader, and your research names three others" is useful. "You should test more angles" is not.

## What not to do

- Do not recommend generating a page to fix something an edit fixes.
- Do not treat a page's existence as evidence it works. You cannot see performance from here. Say so rather than implying a page is a winner.
- Do not propose a full portfolio build-out. Recommend the next page, or the next two.
